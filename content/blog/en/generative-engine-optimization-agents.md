**Generative Engine Optimization (GEO) is the practice of structuring your content so that AI agents and answer engines — ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews, Gemini, Claude — cite you in their answers.** With traditional search click-through rates falling as AI answers replace result lists, GEO is how a small site stays visible to people who never click a blue link. Here's what it is and how to apply it.

## Why GEO exists

For twenty years, the measure of online visibility was simple: rank high on a search results page, get the click. That's changing. When you ask an AI assistant a question, it often answers directly — and if it cites a source, it links it, but many people just read the answer and move on. The click may never happen.

The consequence is that [traditional search volume is declining as people shift toward direct answers](https://www.yotpo.com/blog/generative-engine-optimization-tools/). The marketing research firm Yotpo and others point to the same trend, and Google itself now publishes guidance for [optimizing for its generative AI features](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide). That's the shift in one line: the answer engines are reading your page and deciding whether to cite it — and whether you get cited is a different skill from ranking in results.

## GEO vs. SEO

GEO looks like SEO but has a different goal. SEO aims to win a ranking and get a click; GEO aims to get **cited** in an answer. Practically, that changes what "rank" means — there's no position to watch, only whether the AI mentions or links you when asked the right question.

| | SEO | GEO |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goal | Rank in results | Be cited in an AI answer |
| Metric | Position, click-through rate | Share of voice, citations |
| Audience | A person who clicks | A model that reads and cites |
| Key asset | Keyword hierarchy, backlinks | Clear, sourced, structured content |
| Success looks like | A blue link on page one | Your name in an answer |

The search engine itself now [explains how it decides what to cite for generative AI](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide): it runs a set of concurrent related queries (a "query fan-out") to gather context, then composes an answer. The practical implication is that a model cites content that's easy to retrieve, understand, and extract a clean answer from.

## How to apply GEO

The good news is that GEO favors the same things that make content genuinely useful. You don't need to game a model; you need to make your content the kind that a model can parse and trust.

1. **Answer the core question in the first two sentences.** Answer engines pull from the top of a page. Lead with the answer, then explain. This is the single highest-leverage change.
2. **Structure with headers and crisp sections.** Models retrieve and quote cleanly from content with a clear hierarchy: H2 for each subtopic, short paragraphs.
3. **Include facts and specifics, not just claims.** A model is more likely to cite a page with concrete, checkable information than one with vague assertions. Cite your sources.
4. **Use tables and lists where they fit.** Structured data is easy to extract and reference. Comparing options in a table makes you the obvious citation for "compare X and Y."
5. **Make it citable by name.** A model will say "according to [your name]" if your content is distinct and authoritative. A clear author, a strong take, and a consistent brand all help.

The [industry guides](https://llmrefs.com/generative-engine-optimization) agree on the same fundamentals — and because the bar is "be retrievable and trustworthy," the work compounds rather than chasing an algorithm update.
