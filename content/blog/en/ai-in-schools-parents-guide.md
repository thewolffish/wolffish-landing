**Two things changed in September 2026: New York City put a one-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI from pre-K through 8th grade, and Microsoft signed a national, legally enforceable AI safety and privacy standard with a major teachers union.** Together they set the terms every other district will now be measured against.

Here is what each actually says, and the handful of things worth doing as a parent.

## What New York City did

On **September 2, 2026**, Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels announced [the broadest student-facing AI policy in the country](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/09/mayor-mamdani-and-chancellor-samuels-put-students-first-with-nat):

| Rule | Scope |
| --- | --- |
| One-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI | Grades 2-K through 8 |
| Companion chatbots | Banned in all grades |
| Students affected | Nearly 600,000 — about two-thirds of the system's enrollment |
| AI literacy modules for high schoolers | Two 45-minute classes per year, all students |
| Supervised AI pilots | Max 5 classes per high school, up to 50,000 students (~5%) |
| Teachers using AI for planning | Still permitted, with tool requirements |

The pilot list is short and specific — Quill for close reading, Edia for math reasoning, Brisk Teaching, Playlab, and Intel AI-Ready Schools — each with a stated weekly time cap, under direct supervision of a trained educator.

Two details matter more than the headline. **Companion chatbots are banned in every grade**, including high school — the concern is emotional reliance, not homework cheating. And the district **explicitly kept AI in teachers' hands** for planning and admin work. The ban is about students, not about the technology.

The moratorium runs through the 2026–27 school year. A Technology in Schools Coalition of students, educators, parents, and union partners will convene during it and publish recommendations for what comes next.

## What the Microsoft agreement did

A week later, on **September 9, 2026**, the American Federation of Teachers and Microsoft announced [a national AI safety and privacy standard for schools](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/09/09/aft-union-microsoft-ai-deal-01068544) — notably framed as **legally enforceable**, written into contracts rather than promised in a blog post. It puts the burden on vendors: student data protections, school control over which tools are used, and transparency for parents. Union leaders have said they want OpenAI and Anthropic to sign next.

The two moves are complementary rather than contradictory. New York City restricted *students'* access; the union deal constrains *vendors*. One district can be reversed by the next administration. A contract clause is harder to unwind.

## What parents should actually check

The policy is national news; your child's experience is decided at the building level. Five questions get you most of the way:

1. **Which tools are in use, by name?** Ask for the list, not a category like "AI-powered learning platform."
2. **Is any of it student-facing generative AI?** In New York City it should not be, through 8th grade. Elsewhere, ask what the district's own policy says.
3. **What data leaves the building?** Student names, grades, writing samples, and IEP details are the sensitive set. A compliant tool does not train on them.
4. **Is a chatbot positioned as a companion or counselor?** This is the risk that gets least attention and worries child-development experts most — an always-available, always-agreeable responder is a poor substitute for a person.
5. **Who is accountable when it's wrong?** Ask for the escalation path, in writing.

Used well, it handles the administrative layer parents actually struggle with: the district AI policy PDF nobody reads, the vendor privacy terms, the flyer pile, the teacher email you keep meaning to send. ## What to expect next

Three things to watch over the school year:

- **Whether the pilots produce evidence or marketing.** New York City says it will publish a report; the useful version includes the outcome data, not just the tool list.
- **Whether the union standard spreads.** If OpenAI and Anthropic sign, districts get a template instead of negotiating twenty contracts.
- **Whether state law moves.** A district-level moratorium can be reversed. Legislation cannot be, as easily.

**The takeaway:** you don't need to win the AI debate to protect your kid. Ask five specific questions at your school, keep the AI you control on your side of the homework, and treat any chatbot that wants to be your child's friend as the red flag it is.
