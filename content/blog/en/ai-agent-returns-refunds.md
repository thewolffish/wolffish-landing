Yes — a personal AI agent can take most of a return off your hands, but it cannot make money move faster. The retailer's clock is the retailer's clock. What an agent actually fixes is the part where returns fail: the request that never gets filed, the refund that never gets chased, and the deadline you didn't know was running.

Start with the two deadlines that decide everything. An eligibility window (when you may return) and a processing window (when the money comes back) are different things, and people miss the first while waiting on the second.

## The two clocks

| Clock | Typical window | What it controls |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Return eligibility | 30 days from delivery for most Amazon items, longer in some holiday periods | Whether you can return at all — this one expires |
| Refund processing | Up to 30 days overall on Amazon; card refunds often post 3–5 business days after processing | Whether the money is back — this one just runs |
| Card dispute window | 60 days from the statement showing the problem, under the US Fair Credit Billing Act | Your last resort if the refund never arrives |

The trap is obvious once you name it: an agent that files on day 27 is fine. An agent that helps you *notice* on day 34 is useless. Which means the single highest-value thing an agent does here is not filing returns — it is watching the window.

## What an agent can and can't do

Retailers are automating the merchant side of this heavily, which tells you the workflow is well-defined. The consumer side is mostly still manual, which is where an agent earns its place.

| Step | Agent can do it | You still do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Find the order and the policy | Yes — reads the confirmation email and the return terms | — |
| Check eligibility and the deadline | Yes, and it can warn you days ahead | Decide it's worth returning |
| Draft the return or refund request | Yes, in your words, citing the order number | Approve the send |
| Print the label / book the pickup | Yes, if it has access to the portal | Physically box the item |
| Chase a missing refund | Yes — the follow-up that humans never send | Escalate if it stalls twice |
| Open a card dispute | Drafts it with dates and evidence attached | File it in your name |

The honest limit: portals that only accept a click inside a logged-in session need your real browser, and some retailers deliberately make the button hard to find. That is a tooling problem, not a dead end — it's exactly the case [why agents get blocked on websites](https://wolffi.sh/blog/why-ai-agents-get-blocked-websites), and why a browser session that is already signed in beats a headless one.

## The six-step return run

Give your agent this as one job, not six chats.

1. **Collect the paper trail.** Forward the order confirmation and the return policy to the agent. If it already reads your inbox, have it pull them by order number.
2. **Compute both clocks.** Ask for the last eligible return date and the date the refund should land — as two explicit dates.
3. **File on day one, not day twenty-nine.** The request goes out the moment you decide. Backdate nothing, and never describe an unused item as new if it isn't.
4. **Set two reminders.** One a few days before the eligibility window closes, one on the expected refund date.
5. **Chase quietly, then escalate.** A short, specific follow-up — order number, return tracking, expected amount, date promised — after the first missed date. Escalate publicly or by phone after the second.
6. **Keep the whole thread.** When a refund does go wrong, the difference between a dispute you win and one you lose is a file with dates in it.

That last step compounds. A year of returns turns into a small, searchable record of what you bought, what you sent back, and what you were owed — the same habit that makes [subscription audits](https://wolffi.sh/blog/ai-agent-cancel-subscriptions) work.

## When to escalate to a chargeback

A chargeback is the right move when three things are true: the goods went back, you have tracking, and the merchant has gone quiet past their own stated window. Under the Fair Credit Billing Act you generally have **60 days from the statement that first showed the problem** to dispute the charge in writing with your card issuer — so the clock on escalation starts when the charge posts, not when you get annoyed.

Your agent's job here is assembly, not aggression: a one-page timeline (order date, delivery date, return date, tracking number, promised refund date, contacts), attached to a written dispute. Most issuers will accept it as is. And note which half this is — the dispute is filed by you, in your name. The agent prepared the case.

An agent cannot speed up a refund, but it can make sure you never miss the window, never forget the follow-up, and always have the paper trail when you escalate. Give it the order number, ask for two dates, and let it do the chasing. That's the whole gain — and it's a real one.
