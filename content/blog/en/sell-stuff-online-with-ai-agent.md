**Selling your old stuff online is 10% pricing and 90% typing: titles, descriptions, categories, dimensions, then doing it all again on a second marketplace.** That typing is exactly what AI is now good at, and 2026 is the first year the tools arrived on the platforms where normal people actually sell — Meta's Seller app turns a photo into a finished listing, and eBay's app fills in item details from a photo or a barcode.

Here is the pipeline that gets a spare room cleared, in the order the work actually happens.

## The honest maths of resale

The reason most people never sell their old phone is not that nobody wants it. It is that a listing is fifteen minutes of admin for forty dollars, and a closet is twenty listings. The work that has to happen before money arrives is where the whole thing dies.

That is why to judge any AI tool here by one question: **how much of the listing does it produce without you typing?** Not "does it suggest a price" — does it get you to a publishable listing from a photo.

## Step 1: photograph everything once, in one session

Batch it. Pull everything into one room under good light, put a blank wall or a sheet behind it, and shoot each item on its own — the front, the label, and any damage. Then stop.

This sounds like the non-AI step, and it is the one that decides everything after: AI listing tools read photos, so a photo with three items in it produces three confused listings. Take the twenty photos in one sitting; you will not take them one at a time over three weeks.

## Step 2: price from sold data, not asking prices

This is where people lose money in both directions. Asking prices are fiction — anyone can list a scratched laptop at $900. What something is worth is what comparable items **sold** for, recently, in your market.

Meta's approach is a price suggestion drawn from similar items listed near you. eBay shows sold comps directly in search results, which is the closest thing to ground truth, and it is what experienced resellers price against. Cross-listing tools such as Voolist go a step further and suggest the right category per destination platform, which matters because the same item belongs in different buckets on eBay and on Facebook Marketplace.

Whatever number you get, subtract for condition and add for accessories. Original box and charger are worth real money on a used phone and almost nothing on a used blender.

## Step 3: let the AI write the listing, then edit two lines

Meta's Seller app takes your uploaded photos and has Meta AI fill in the title, description, price suggestion and category, with a bulk-listing mode for doing several at once. eBay's app does the same from a photo, and its barcode-scanning flow fills item details for anything with a UPC. eBay has said the image-based tool launched as iOS-only, so check what your platform currently supports before planning a workflow around it.

Then do the two edits AI will not do for you:

- **Add the flaw, in plain words.** "Cracked corner, shown in photo 3." Buyers forgive damage that is disclosed and punish damage that is discovered.
- **Cut the adjectives.** "Gorgeous must-have" reads as a red flag. "iPhone 14, 128GB, unlocked, 87% battery health" reads as a seller who knows what they have.

## Step 4: cross-list, because buyers are not on one platform

The same item sells for different money in different places, and listing twice by hand is where the fifteen minutes becomes thirty. This is the step where a tool pays for itself.

| Approach | Good for | The catch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sell on one platform only | A single item, fast | You accept that platform's price |
| Native AI listing tool (Seller, eBay app) | Photos, drafts, bulk listing | Locked to one marketplace |
| Cross-listing software | Same item, several marketplaces | Usually a subscription; relisting rules vary |
| Your own AI agent driving the browser | Any marketplace, plus negotiation | Needs setup and permissions you control |

The last row is what a personal agent is for, and it is where the market is moving: some newer services attach an agent to each individual listing so it answers buyer questions and evaluates offers. Treat those as promising rather than proven — but the direction is clear, and the queue of "is this still available?" messages is the single most automatable part of the whole job.

## Step 5: handle the messages with rules, not vibes

The unified inbox is now standard — Meta's Seller app threads every buyer conversation by item, so you can see the whole history for one listing in one place.

Decide your rules before the messages arrive: your floor price, what you will not negotiate on, whether you ship or meet only, and how you take payment. Then automate the repetitive part — the availability answer, the "yes it's still $120", the pickup window — and keep the human part human. If you find yourself negotiating hard on a $30 item, you have already lost more in time than the item is worth.

## What to keep human

- **Meeting up.** Every scam story in second-hand selling starts with a stranger and a payment method you should not have accepted. Cash, in public, in daylight.
- **Anything with a serial number or personal data.** Wipe devices properly, and check what [AI agents can and can't be trusted to buy or handle](https://wolffi.sh/blog/ai-agents-buy-things-for-you) before you hand an agent your marketplace login.
- **Rare or high-value items.** AI pricing is built on comparables; when there are none, the tool will confidently guess.

## The takeaway

The AI does not change what your stuff is worth — it changes how much admin stands between you and the money. Photograph in one batch, price against sold listings rather than asking prices, let the listing write itself and fix the two lines that matter, cross-list with a tool, and put rules in front of the message queue. A closet clears in a weekend instead of never.
