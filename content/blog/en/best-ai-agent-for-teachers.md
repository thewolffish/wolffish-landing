The best AI agent for a teacher is not the one with the biggest model — it is the one that removes preparation work while keeping you in control of what reaches students. The preparation-versus-delivery split is the whole story here, and the data on how teachers actually use AI says the same thing.

## Teachers already use AI — for preparation

The adoption numbers are no longer in question. A survey by the marketplace TPT found that [80% of educators reported using generative AI tools](https://thejournal.com/articles/2026/04/22/80-of-teachers-are-using-ai-tools-in-the-classroom.aspx), with 58% using them regularly or occasionally and 22% having tried them once or twice. That is not a fringe behaviour; it is the default.

What matters more is *what they use it for*. In the Gallup–Walton Family Foundation survey of more than 1,000 teachers, the top applications were [research and content gathering (44%), creating lesson plans (38%), summarising information (38%), and generating classroom materials (37%)](https://www.engageli.com/blog/ai-in-education-statistics). Delivery and marking barely register by comparison. As one analysis of the same data puts it, prep use outruns marking use by roughly seven to one.

That ratio is the design brief. A teacher does not need an AI that teaches the class. A teacher needs an AI that handles the preparation stack — and that stack is where a chatbot runs out of road.

## Why a chatbot is not enough

A chatbot answers. It does not, on its own:

- Remember that you teach Grade 7, that Unit 3 follows Unit 2, or that your class has four students on modified plans.
- Hold your curriculum framework and your school's template.
- Work through the week while you sleep and hand you Thursday's materials on Wednesday.
- Keep a version history of what you changed and why.

Those four things are the difference between an assistant you prompt and an agent you run. The distinction is the same one we drew in [what makes an agent different from a chatbot](https://wolffi.sh/blog/what-is-a-personal-ai-agent), and it matters most in exactly this kind of recurring, templated work.

## How to choose one

| What you need | What to look for | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lesson planning | Access to your curriculum documents and a template | It should fill your format, not invent one |
| Differentiation | Per-student notes it can read | Otherwise every task comes back at one level |
| Marking support | Rubrics it must follow, with reasons | You want traceable, not confident |
| Admin | Email and calendar access | Timetabling and parent messages are pure overhead |
| Parent communication | Draft-only mode | Nothing about a family should be auto-sent |
| Privacy | A clear answer on where student data goes | This is the one that gets tools banned |

That last row is not a formality. Only a minority of schools have a written AI policy, which means the practical rule is being set by individual teachers in individual classrooms — a situation that is fine until it is not. If you are the one introducing the tool, write down what it is allowed to see before you use it, and check it against your district's guidance.

## A setup that works in four steps

1. **Load the curriculum, not the internet.** Point the agent at your syllabus, your unit templates, and your assessment rubrics. A teacher's agent is only as useful as the documents it can read.
2. **Template everything.** One prompt per recurring artefact — a lesson plan, a quiz, a parent email, a set of differentiated tasks. A template you reuse is worth more than a clever prompt you use once.
3. **Keep student data out of it.** Use placeholders for names. The agent writing "Student A needs a scaffolded version" is exactly as useful as one writing "Ahmed", and it is dramatically safer.
4. **Schedule the recurring parts.** A weekly run that drafts next week's materials and puts them where you look is where the time actually comes back. Automations are covered in our [scheduling guide](https://wolffi.sh/blog/schedule-ai-agent-automations).

If you want this without sending your curriculum to a third party, a [local-first agent](https://wolffi.sh/start) keeps the documents and the memory on your own machine and only calls a model for the reasoning — the same trade-off we compared in [local versus cloud assistants](https://wolffi.sh/blog/local-first-vs-cloud-ai-assistant).

## What to watch out for

Three failure modes specific to education:

- **Plausible nonsense in subject matter.** Generated questions can be subtly wrong, and a wrong answer key costs more time than writing one. Read every artefact before it reaches a student.
- **Generic output.** An agent without your class's context produces the same worksheet thousands of other teachers got. The context *is* the value.
- **Drift into grading decisions.** AI can prepare rubrics, sort evidence, and draft feedback. The judgement call should stay yours, both pedagogically and legally.

There is also a student-side reason to be deliberate: your students are using AI at a higher rate than you are. What you model in your own workflow — cite it, check it, keep your judgement — is the curriculum they will actually absorb.

## Takeaway

Pick an agent that reads your curriculum, holds your templates, and works while you sleep — then keep it out of student data and out of delivery. The adoption is already here; the differentiator is whether the tool takes the preparation stack off your plate or just adds another thing to supervise.
